# Why Our Country Loaf Takes Twenty Hours

In January 2025, we stopped using commercial yeast. Every loaf we bake now rises on a sourdough starter, a live culture of wild yeast and bacteria that we feed flour and water every day. The starter came from my grandmother's crock, which she started in 1962 and kept going for decades. It's a little piece of her kitchen that's still working.

I'll be honest about what that switch cost us. A loaf made with commercial yeast takes about four hours from mixing to oven. Our sourdough takes about twenty. The dough ferments slowly, overnight and into the next day, and that slowness is where the flavor comes from. It also meant I had to rethink nearly everything about how we run the bakery: when we mix, how we schedule shifts, how much dough we can plan for on any given day.

For four months, we lost money. We kept only three of our eleven breads, the ones that worked best with the slower process. I sometimes wondered whether I had made a mistake.

Then something changed. Our country loaf, with its crackly crust, open crumb, and tang that mellows as it sits, started selling more than we expected. Weekly sales went from about 60 loaves to 140 by September. People started coming back for it, and then they started telling their friends.

One of our longtime customers, who lives with diabetes, told me he feels his body handles our sourdough better than other bread he's tried. I'm a baker, not a doctor, so I won't make claims about what it does for anyone's health. I'll only say I was glad to hear he enjoys it, and I'd encourage anyone with questions about their diet to talk with their own doctor.

What I can tell you is what the bread tastes like. It's dense enough to hold up to butter and jam, sour enough to make you pause, and it keeps well for days.

If you haven't tried it yet, come see us on Saturday mornings. We pull the country loaves out of the oven early, and they're at their best when they're still warm. Stop by Kessler's Bread on Main Street in Sidney, grab a loaf, and taste what twenty hours of patience gets you.

*Ruth Kessler*
*Kessler's Bread, Sidney, Ohio*
