# Finishing a Walnut Dining Table: A Slow Build Pays Off

There's a moment in every finish schedule when you stop trusting your eyes and start trusting the process. For our latest commission, a solid black walnut dining table for a family of six, that moment came about three days into finishing, when the wood had gone from flat and dull to something that looked like it had been lit from within.

This is the story of how we finished it, and why we made the choices we did.

## Starting With the Wood

Black walnut is one of the most forgiving and rewarding species we work with. It machines cleanly, takes stain predictably, and ages into a deep, warm brown. But it also has quirks. The sapwood is pale and will look unfinished next to the heartwood unless you deal with it deliberately. And walnut's open grain means you can't skip filling if you want a glass-smooth surface.

Before any finish touched this top, we spent two full days on preparation:

- **Sanding progression.** We went 120, 150, 180, then 220 grit, checking under raking light between each step. Skipping grits leaves scratches that stain will highlight, so we didn't skip.
- **Raising the grain.** We wiped the surface with distilled water, let it dry, and lightly sanded again with 220. This keeps the finish from fighting a surface that swells after the first coat.
- **Grain filling.** Walnut's pores are large, so we filled them with a paste filler tinted to match the heartwood. Thin applications, worked across the grain, then sanded back flush.

## Choosing the Finish

We wanted a finish that would protect a table used daily by children, dinner guests, and the occasional spilled glass of red wine, while still letting the walnut look like walnut. We settled on a hardwax oil for the base, followed by a wiping varnish topcoat for added durability at the surface.

The reasoning was simple. Hardwax oil penetrates the fibers and gives a natural, tactile feel that film finishes can't match. Wiping varnish builds a thin protective layer without the thick, plastic look of spray lacquer. Together, they give you the warmth of oil and the resilience of a film.

## The Schedule

Our finishing schedule ran over five days:

**Day 1: Base coat.** We applied a thin coat of hardwax oil, flooded the surface, and wiped off the excess after fifteen minutes. Walnut drinks oil quickly, so we watched for dry patches and fed them more as needed.

**Day 2: Cure and inspect.** The first coat needed to fully set. We checked the surface under raking light, looking for blotches and uneven absorption. Where the grain had pulled more oil, we lightly scuffed and reapplied.

**Day 3: Build coats.** Two thin coats of wiping varnish went on, each one sanded lightly with 320 grit once dry. This is where the table started to glow. The varnish deepened the figure in the grain, and the color shifted from dusty brown to rich chocolate with amber undertones.

**Day 4: Final coat and rubbing.** One more varnish coat, then we let it cure for a full day before lightly rubbing the surface with 0000 steel wool and a paste wax. The rubbing removed any dust nibs and left a soft, low-sheen finish that resists fingerprints.

**Day 5: Hardware and final inspection.** We reattached the aprons, installed the leg hardware, and checked the finish one last time under daylight and under lamp light. Both showed clean, even color.

## What We'd Do Differently

No project is perfect. On this one, we'd have built in an extra day between coats. Rushing the second varnish layer left a faint orange peel in one corner, which we fixed with a careful rub-out, but it cost us a few hours we could have avoided. Patience with curing times is the cheapest insurance a finisher can buy.

## Caring for Your Walnut Table

If you own a finished walnut table, here's how to keep it looking its best:

- Wipe spills promptly with a soft, dry cloth.
- Use coasters and placemats for hot dishes and cold glasses.
- Avoid harsh cleaners. A damp cloth and mild soap are enough.
- Refresh the finish with a light coat of hardwax oil every year or two, especially in high-traffic areas.
