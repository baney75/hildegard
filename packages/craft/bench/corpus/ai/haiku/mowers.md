# Don't Let Your Mower Wreck Your Spring: Winterizing Tips from Dale's Small Engine

Every spring, we see the same problem over and over. A mower that ran fine last fall won't start, sputters when it does, or runs rough and dies under load. About 80% of the spring repairs that come through our shop are gummed-up carburetors, and nearly all of them trace back to old gas left sitting in the tank over the winter.

## Why Gas Goes Bad

Most gasoline sold today contains ethanol, and ethanol attracts moisture and breaks down over time. Gas with ethanol can start to go bad in about 30 days. As it degrades, it leaves varnish and gummy deposits in the carburetor's tiny jets and passages. Those deposits block fuel flow, and no amount of pulling the starter cord will fix that. The carburetor has to be cleaned or rebuilt.

A carburetor rebuild at our shop costs $85 to $120. A bottle of fuel stabilizer costs about $8. The math favors doing a little prep in the fall.

## Two Ways to Protect Your Engine

**Option 1: Run it dry.** Run the engine until it stops on its own, so no fuel remains in the carburetor bowl or lines. This is a good choice if you won't be using the mower again until spring.

**Option 2: Use fuel stabilizer.** Fill the tank with fresh gas, add stabilizer according to the bottle's directions, and run the engine for about 5 minutes. That circulates the treated fuel through the carburetor so it's protected. Then store the mower with the tank full, which also limits moisture buildup in the tank.

Either way, avoid leaving old gas in the tank over winter. If you have gas that's more than a month old, drain it at a proper disposal site or use it up before storage.

## Safety First: Disconnect the Spark Plug Wire

Before you touch the blade for any reason, pull the spark plug wire off the plug and move it out of the way. This keeps the engine from starting accidentally while your hands are near the blade. It takes five seconds and prevents serious injuries. Make this a habit every time you work on the mower.

## Get Your Blade Sharpened Before Winter

A dull blade tears grass instead of cutting it, which leaves ragged, brown tips and makes your lawn more vulnerable to disease. Winter is the ideal time to have the blade sharpened, so it's ready to go in spring.
