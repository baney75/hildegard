# The Shepherd Who Goes Looking

Imagine a shepherd standing at the edge of the hillside at dusk. He has ninety-nine sheep safe in the fold, and he has counted them. One is missing. Any sensible person would ask whether ninety-nine out of a hundred is a good outcome. Jesus asks his listeners to picture something else: the shepherd leaves the ninety-nine and goes out into the dark to find the one.

Luke tells this story in the fifteenth chapter, and it opens a window onto the heart of God. The tax collectors and sinners were drawing near to hear Jesus, and the religious leaders were grumbling that he welcomed them and ate with them. So Jesus told them three parables about things that were lost: a sheep, a coin, and a son. In each one, something of value goes missing, and in each one, someone searches until the lost thing is found. Then comes the celebration.

Notice what the shepherd does not do. He does not wait for the sheep to find its own way home. A sheep is not a creature of good sense. It wanders, it grazes its way farther from the flock, and it often does not realize it is lost at all. The shepherd does not blame the sheep for being foolish, and he does not shrug and count the rest. He goes looking, and he keeps looking until he finds it.

When he finds it, he does not drag it back in anger. He lifts it onto his shoulders and carries it home. Then he calls his friends and neighbors and says, "Rejoice with me, for I have found my sheep that was lost." Jesus says there is more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents than over ninety-nine righteous people who need no repentance.

Some of us hear that and feel uneasy. Are we the ninety-nine, safe and comfortable, doing what we are told and wondering why the shepherd spends so much energy on someone who strayed? Or are we the one, lost in some field we wandered into, unsure how we got there and afraid to be found? Most of us are both at different times. The good news is that the story does not ask us to choose a side. It tells us about the shepherd, and about the kind of God who will leave the flock to find us.

Think about what the parable says about worth. The sheep was one of a hundred, and to a practical bookkeeper, losing one might seem a reasonable cost. But the shepherd does not measure value by the total. Each sheep is known by name, and each one matters. God does not see us as a statistic in a census. God knows us individually, and no one is too far off to be sought.

It also says something about the cost of finding. Searching a hillside at night is hard, slow, and sometimes dangerous. The shepherd accepts that cost willingly. Jesus, the one who tells this story, would later lay down his life for the sheep. The good shepherd in this parable points toward the one who went all the way to the cross so that no one would be left out in the dark.

So where does that leave us this morning? If you feel far from home, whether through a choice you regret, a season of doubt, or a sense that you have drifted past the reach of grace, hear this: you are not too lost to be found. The shepherd is still searching, and he is not angry. He is looking for you, and when he finds you, he will carry you home with joy.

And if you are among the ninety-nine, comfortable in the fold, take up the shepherd's work. Look for the one who has wandered. Notice the person who has stopped coming, the neighbor who is struggling, the stranger who has never felt welcome. Go after them with patience and without judgment.

Let us pray. Good Shepherd, you know each of us by name. Thank you for seeking us when we wandered, and for carrying us home when we could not find the way. Help us to rest in your care, and help us to join you in the search for those still lost. Amen.
