# Sitting on the Porch

Luke 15 begins with a complaint. Tax collectors and sinners were gathering around Jesus to hear him, and the Pharisees and scribes muttered, "This man welcomes sinners and eats with them." So Jesus told them stories, three of them, one after another. We know the first two well: a shepherd with a hundred sheep who loses one and leaves the ninety-nine to search, and a woman with ten silver coins who loses one, lights a lamp, and sweeps the whole house until she finds it.

I want to spend a minute on the coin, because we usually skip past it. A sheep wanders. It gets distracted by a patch of grass. A coin doesn't do anything. It's lost because it was dropped, or swept under a cabinet, or it's just sitting in the dark where no one thought to look. Nobody in that story is to blame for the coin being lost. And still she searches as if the whole house depended on it.

Last month, a young man from our youth group walked away from this church. Some of you know the reason. His father's funeral was the week before, and afterward he stopped coming. Not dramatically. He stopped answering texts, then stopped showing up on Wednesday nights, and then his chair was empty for a month.

On a Tuesday evening, three of us drove to his apartment. I won't pretend we had a plan. We had a car, a bag of gas station donuts, and the stubborn hope that showing up might count for something. We knocked. He opened the door, looked at us, and went back inside. So we sat down on his porch, and we stayed about an hour. The cold came up through the boards. He never came back out, and he never said a word to us. I'm not sure he ever knew how long we stayed or why.

We didn't say much either. We didn't have a speech, and I've learned that speeches are usually for the person giving them. Mostly we sat there, which is harder than it sounds. We were waiting, and we were also just present, in a place where he couldn't pretend nobody cared.

Two weeks later he walked into youth group as if he had never left. He sat in the back. He didn't explain anything, and nobody asked him to. That's the part I keep returning to.

I don't want to tie this up with a neat bow. It would be easy to say that God searches for us the way that shepherd and that woman searched, that the story ends happily, and then go home. But the parable doesn't end with the search. It ends with a party. The shepherd calls his friends, and the woman calls her neighbors. Heaven rejoices, and I believe that. But notice that the joy comes after the searching, and the searching is slow and unglamorous. It involves a lot of dust, a lot of sweeping, and a lot of sitting in the dark, not knowing whether anything you do will matter.

Some of you are on a porch right now. You have someone you love who has gone quiet. You've knocked, and the door has closed, and you wonder whether it's worth going back. What these parables suggest is that the one who searches doesn't have to find the right words. You only have to keep the lamp lit and keep sweeping.

Jesus told these stories to people who were grumbling about who was welcome. His answer was that the shepherd does not count the cost of going after what is lost, and the woman does not decide the coin is too small to bother with. That is God's posture toward those who have wandered, and it is the posture he asks of us.

So here is what I'm sure of. The young man who sat in our back row did not come back because of a good argument. He came back because someone refused to give up on him, and because this church's door was still open when he was ready to walk through it. Let's keep it open. Let's keep going back to the porch.
