# Finishing a Walnut Dining Table: How We Do It and Why

Walnut sells itself. Chocolate heartwood, streaks of purple and gold, figure that moves when you walk past it. It's one of the few domestic hardwoods that people ask for by name. It's also easy to finish badly. A walnut table can come out of the shop looking flat, muddy, or plasticky, and the cause is almost always the finish.

A dining table is the hardest-working piece of furniture in most homes. It sees hot plates, sweating glasses, spilled wine, homework, elbows, and decades of wiping. Here's how we finish ours so they look like walnut on the first day and still hold up twenty years later.

## Start With the Surface

The finish only magnifies what's under it. Any scratch, glue smear, or swirl mark you can barely see in raw wood will stand out once the oil goes on.

**Sand progressively.** We flatten the top first, then sand through 80, 120, 150, and 180 grit. We usually stop at 180 for walnut tops. Going much finer can burnish the surface and keep oil from penetrating evenly. Don't skip grits: each one has to remove the scratches the previous one left.

**Check with raking light.** Between grits we hold a work light low across the surface. Scratches show up under raking light that you'd never see from overhead. A quick wipe with mineral spirits helps too, since it previews how the wood will look under finish.

**Raise the grain.** If the finish is water-based, or the table will get damp-rag cleaning (every dining table does), we wipe the surface with a lightly damp cloth, let it dry, and knock the raised fibers down with a final pass at 180 or 220. This keeps the first spill from turning the top fuzzy.

**Ease the edges.** A slight roundover or chamfer on every edge helps the finish build properly there. Finish pulls away from sharp corners, and those corners are where wear starts.

## Don't Stain Walnut

People sometimes ask us to darken a walnut table, and we nearly always talk them out of it. Stain sits in the pores and blurs the color variation that makes walnut beautiful. If someone wants a deeper tone, the right oil-based finish gets most of the way there while keeping the wood looking like wood.

Some customers ask about the creamy sapwood along the edges of a board. We decide on that when we select the lumber, not at the finishing stage. A little sapwood can be a feature. If it isn't wanted, we cut it out. We don't paint over it.

## Choosing the Finish

No finish is perfect for every table. Each one trades off beauty, durability, and how easy it is to repair. These are the options we use and when we use them.

### Oil-Based Wiping Varnish

This is our usual choice for dining tables. A wiping varnish is an oil-based polyurethane or alkyd varnish thinned so it can be wiped on in thin coats instead of brushed. Oil-based finishes bring out walnut's depth and give it a slight warmth. After enough coats the film resists water, alcohol, and heat well.

We wipe on a thin coat, let it cure overnight, scuff lightly with a gray abrasive pad or 320-grit paper, and repeat. A tabletop typically gets five to seven coats. The base and underside get fewer. The result is a durable surface that looks like satin rather than plastic.

### Hardwax Oil

Hardwax oils such as Rubio Monocoat and Osmo Polyx penetrate the wood and cure into the top fibers. They leave very little film. The look is beautiful and natural, the closest you can get to bare, oiled walnut, and the feel under your hand is hard to beat.

The tradeoff is that hardwax oils are less protective against standing liquids and heat than a built-up varnish. Their big advantage is easy repair. A worn or damaged spot can be cleaned and re-oiled locally without stripping the whole top. This suits families who don't mind a table that shows some life and who'd rather do a ten-minute touch-up than a full refinish.

### Water-Based Finishes

Modern water-based polyurethanes are tough and clear, and they don't yellow. On light woods like maple, that's exactly what you want. On walnut they can look cold and slightly gray, as though the wood has been washed out. If we use a water-based topcoat on walnut, we put down a coat of oil or dewaxed shellac first to bring back the warmth.

### What About Pure Oil?

Straight tung oil, linseed oil, and "Danish oil" blends look lovely on walnut, and we use them on smaller pieces. On a dining table we don't use them alone. They offer very little protection against water rings and food stains, and they need regular reapplication.

## Grain Filling: Optional, Not Required

Walnut has open pores. Under a thin finish you'll see and feel the texture of the grain, and most of our customers like that. Anyone who wants a glassy, mirror-flat surface can get one by grain-filling first, using either a paste filler or several coats of finish sanded back into the pores. It adds work and gives a more formal look. For most dining tables we leave the pores open.

## Finish Both Sides

This step is skipped more than any other, and skipping it causes the most trouble. Wood takes on and gives off moisture with the seasons. If the top of a table is sealed and the underside is bare, the two faces move at different rates and the top can cup.

We put finish on the underside, the aprons, and the inside faces of the legs. These areas don't need as many coats as the top, but they need some. Moisture exchange should be roughly even on all surfaces.

## Patience at the End

A finish can feel dry in a few hours and still be far from cured. Oil-based varnishes and hardwax oils keep hardening for weeks. Our advice to customers:

- **Use the table gently for the first two to four weeks.** Meals are fine. Leaving placemats, tablecloths, or heavy objects on it for long periods isn't.
- **Don't apply furniture polish or wax** during that time, and don't use silicone-based sprays at all. They can interfere with future repairs.
- **Expect the color to change.** Walnut lightens and turns slightly golden with light exposure over the first year. This is normal and part of the wood's character. Move centerpieces around occasionally so you don't end up with a dark shadow in the middle of the top.

## Living With Your Table

Day to day, a walnut table needs very little. Wipe it with a soft, damp cloth and dry it. Use trivets under hot dishes and coasters under drinks that sweat. Steer clear of harsh cleaners, ammonia, and anything abrasive.

A varnished table can go many years before it needs attention, and when it does, a light scuff and a fresh topcoat usually bring it back. A hardwax oil table needs a maintenance coat every year or two, depending on use. It's a job you can do yourself in an afternoon.

A walnut dining table should outlast the people who first sit at it. Most of what lets it do that is careful finishing. If you have questions about the finish on a table we've built for you, or you're planning one of your own, stop by the shop. We're always happy to talk walnut.
