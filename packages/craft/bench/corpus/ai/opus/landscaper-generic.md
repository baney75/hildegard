# Your Fall Lawn Care Checklist: How to Set Up a Greener Spring

When the temperatures drop and the leaves start falling, it's tempting to put the mower away and forget about the yard until April. But fall is one of the most important seasons for your lawn. What you do now affects how your grass survives winter and how quickly it greens up in spring.

The good news is that fall lawn care isn't complicated. Here is what we recommend to our customers every year.

## 1. Keep Mowing (But Adjust the Height)

Grass keeps growing until the first hard frost, so keep mowing as long as it needs it. As the season winds down, gradually lower your mower blade over the last two or three cuts. Aim for about 2 to 2.5 inches for the final mow of the year.

Why it matters: Grass left too long can mat down under snow and invite snow mold and other fungal diseases. Grass cut too short is more exposed to cold and drying winds. Moderate height is the safe middle ground.

**Tip:** Never remove more than one-third of the blade length in a single mow, even in fall.

## 2. Don't Let Leaves Pile Up

A few scattered leaves won't hurt anything. A thick, wet layer blocks sunlight, traps moisture, and smothers the grass underneath. That can leave dead, bare patches come spring.

You have a few options:

- **Mulch them.** Running over a light layer of dry leaves with your mower chops them into small pieces that break down and feed the soil.
- **Rake or blow them.** For heavy leaf cover, remove them regularly instead of waiting for every tree to drop.
- **Compost them.** Shredded leaves make excellent compost for garden beds.

Staying on top of leaves every week or two is much easier than tackling one giant pile in December.

## 3. Aerate Compacted Soil

Over the summer, foot traffic, kids, pets, and mowing compact the soil. Compacted soil makes it hard for water, air, and nutrients to reach grass roots.

Core aeration pulls small plugs of soil out of the lawn, opening up space for roots to breathe and grow. Early to mid-fall is ideal for cool-season grasses like Kentucky bluegrass, tall fescue, and perennial ryegrass, because the grass is actively growing and recovers quickly.

Leave the soil plugs on the lawn. They break down on their own within a couple of weeks.

## 4. Overseed Thin or Bare Spots

If your lawn looks patchy after a hot, dry summer, fall is the best time to fix it. Warm soil helps seed germinate, cooler air reduces stress on new grass, and there's less competition from weeds.

Overseeding right after aeration gives you the best results because the seed falls into the holes and makes direct contact with the soil. Keep newly seeded areas consistently moist until the grass is established.

If it's already late in the season where you live, ask us whether dormant seeding is a better option for your lawn.

## 5. Fertilize for Root Strength

A fall fertilizer application is one of the best things you can do for a cool-season lawn. In autumn, grass shifts its energy from top growth to root growth. Feeding it now helps it build strong roots and store nutrients for winter, which leads to a faster, healthier green-up in spring.

Look for a fertilizer designed for fall use, and apply it before the ground freezes. Always follow label directions and local regulations. Some areas restrict fertilizer use near waterways or after certain dates.

**A note on warm-season grasses:** If your lawn is Bermuda, zoysia, St. Augustine, or centipede, the approach is different. These grasses go dormant in fall, and heavy late-season nitrogen can do more harm than good. If you're not sure what type of grass you have, we're happy to take a look.

## 6. Tackle Weeds Now

Broadleaf weeds like dandelions and clover are pulling nutrients down into their roots in fall, just like your grass. That makes this a great time to treat them, since weed control products are carried down into the root system more effectively.

Treating weeds in fall means fewer of them popping up next spring. Spot-treat problem areas, and avoid applying weed control on newly seeded sections until the new grass has been mowed a few times.

## 7. Water Until the Ground Freezes

Cooler weather means less evaporation, but your lawn still needs moisture, especially if you've just seeded or fertilized. If you're not getting about an inch of rain per week, give your lawn a deep watering.

Before the first hard freeze, shut off and drain your irrigation system to prevent burst pipes and damaged sprinkler heads. A professional blowout is the safest way to clear the lines completely.

## 8. Clean Up and Store Your Equipment

Once the last mow is done, give your equipment some attention:

- Clean grass clippings and debris off the mower deck
- Sharpen the blade so it's ready for spring
- Follow the manufacturer's instructions for storing fuel or running the engine dry
- Store hoses indoors so they don't crack

A little maintenance now saves you a frustrating first mow next year.
