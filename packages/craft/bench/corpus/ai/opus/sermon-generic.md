# The Shepherd Who Goes Looking

*A reflection on Luke 15:1–7*

"Now the tax collectors and sinners were all gathering around to hear Jesus. But the Pharisees and the teachers of the law muttered, 'This man welcomes sinners and eats with them.'"

That's how the story begins. Before Jesus says a word about sheep, there's a complaint, and it's a complaint about the company he keeps. The religious leaders can't understand why someone who claims to speak for God would spend his evenings at tables with people everyone else had written off.

So Jesus tells them a story.

"Suppose one of you has a hundred sheep and loses one of them. Doesn't he leave the ninety-nine in the open country and go after the lost sheep until he finds it?"

Notice how Jesus frames the question. He doesn't ask whether this is wise. He asks it as if the answer were obvious: *of course* you would go. Anyone who has loved something precious knows that feeling. When a child wanders off in a crowded store, nobody stops to calculate whether one child out of three is worth the trouble. You drop everything and you search.

But if we're honest, the shepherd's choice is a little reckless. Ninety-nine sheep left in open country is a real risk. A careful manager would cut his losses and protect what remained. One percent is an acceptable margin of error.

The good news of this parable is that God does not run a ledger like that. To God, you are not a percentage. You are not a margin of error. You are the one worth leaving everything to find.

Consider the sheep for a moment. Sheep don't usually get lost on purpose. They nibble their way from one patch of grass to the next, head down, until they look up and nothing is familiar. Many of us know that kind of lostness. We didn't set out to drift from God, from our families, or from the people we hoped we'd become. It happened one small step at a time, until we looked up and wondered how we got here.

And here is what matters most: the sheep does not find its way home. The sheep is found. The parable says nothing about the sheep repenting, apologizing, or cleaning itself up first. The shepherd does the searching. The shepherd does the carrying. "When he finds it, he joyfully puts it on his shoulders and goes home."

That's a picture of grace. We're not saved because we were clever enough to navigate back. We're saved because Someone came looking and would not stop "until he finds it."

Then comes the party. The shepherd calls his friends and neighbors together: "Rejoice with me; I have found my lost sheep." Jesus adds, "In the same way there will be more rejoicing in heaven over one sinner who repents than over ninety-nine righteous persons who do not need to repent."

This is where the story turns back toward the people who were muttering. They are invited to the celebration too. The question the parable leaves hanging is whether they will join it or stand outside with their arms crossed.

That question is for us as well. Some of us are the lost sheep, and we need to hear that we are being sought right now, today, with joy rather than irritation. Others of us have been in the fold a long time. For us, the challenge is to share the Shepherd's heart. Who in our community has wandered? Who has been written off? Whose name do we mutter when we ought to be calling it in welcome?

A church that understands this parable will be a searching church: one that leaves the comfort of the familiar to go find the one who is missing, and one that throws a party when they come home.

May we be found. And may we join the Shepherd in the finding.

Amen.
