# The Shepherd Who Goes Looking

*A reflection on Luke 15:1–7*

Some stories in Scripture are so familiar that we stop hearing them. The parable of the lost sheep is one of them. We picture a soft-focus painting: a gentle man in a robe, a fluffy lamb draped across his shoulders, green hills behind. It is a lovely image. But if we let it stay only lovely, we miss how strange and how startling this story really is.

Luke tells us why Jesus told it. "Now the tax collectors and sinners were all drawing near to hear him. And the Pharisees and the scribes grumbled, saying, 'This man receives sinners and eats with them.'" The parable is an answer to a complaint. The religious people were troubled by the company Jesus kept. So Jesus asked them a question: "What man of you, having a hundred sheep, if he has lost one of them, does not leave the ninety-nine in the open country, and go after the one that is lost, until he finds it?"

Notice that Jesus assumes everyone listening would do the same. But would they? Ninety-nine sheep is a lot of livelihood to leave in the wilderness. Any sensible accountant would say cut your losses. One percent is an acceptable rate of loss. Protect the majority.

But God is not a sensible accountant. God is a shepherd, and a shepherd knows each sheep by name.

That is the first thing this parable teaches us: **you are not a statistic to God.** In a world that measures people by productivity, followers, and usefulness, it is easy to believe that if you slipped away, no one would really notice. The parable says otherwise. Heaven notices. The Shepherd counts, comes up one short, and does not shrug. He goes.

The second thing is this: **the Shepherd does the searching.** The sheep in this story does not find its way home. It does not clean itself up, rehearse an apology, or prove it deserves to be carried back. Sheep, frankly, are not known for their sense of direction. The shepherd goes "until he finds it." That little word *until* carries the whole gospel. God's pursuit of us does not have a time limit or a cutoff. If you feel far away today, hear this clearly: you are not waiting on yourself to be good enough. You are being sought.

The third thing is the part we often skip: **the Shepherd rejoices.** "When he has found it, he lays it on his shoulders, rejoicing." There is no lecture on the walk home. No "I told you so." He carries the weight of the sheep himself, and he is glad to do it. Then he calls his friends and neighbors together for a party. Jesus says, "Just so, I tell you, there will be more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents than over ninety-nine righteous persons who need no repentance."

Which brings us back to the grumblers. The parable gently asks those of us who have been in the fold a long time a hard question: when someone comes home, do we join the celebration, or do we stand at the edge of it with our arms crossed? It is possible to be safe inside the pen and still miss the heart of the Shepherd.

So, church, here is the invitation. If you are the one who has wandered, know that you are wanted, and that the One looking for you is not angry but eager. And if you are among the ninety-nine, let the Shepherd's joy become your own. Make room at the table. Learn the names of the newcomers. Go looking, the way you were looked for.

Because every one of us, at some point, was carried home on someone's shoulders. That is the story of grace. And it is still being told, one lost sheep at a time.

*Amen.*
