# Keep Your Car Starting All Winter: A Practical Guide to Battery Care

Every year, the first hard freeze brings the same wave of phone calls: "My car won't start." In most cases, the culprit is the battery. Cold weather is tough on batteries, but with a little attention you can avoid being stranded on a frozen morning.

## Why Cold Weather Is So Hard on Batteries

A car battery produces power through a chemical reaction, and cold temperatures slow that reaction down. At 32°F, a battery can lose about a third of its starting power. At 0°F, it can lose roughly half. At the same time, cold engine oil thickens, so your engine needs more power to turn over just when your battery has less to give.

Summer heat does the real damage, wearing down a battery's internal components over months. Winter is when that hidden damage finally shows. A battery that limped through August often fails in January.

## Know the Warning Signs

Watch for these symptoms before the battery gives out completely:

- **Slow, sluggish cranking.** The engine turns over more slowly than usual, or makes a labored "rur-rur-rur" sound.
- **Dim headlights or interior lights**, especially when starting the car.
- **A clicking sound** when you turn the key or press the start button.
- **The battery or check-engine warning light** on your dashboard.
- **Corrosion** on the terminals, which looks like white, blue, or green crusty buildup.
- **Age.** Most batteries last three to five years. If yours is in that range, it deserves a test.

## Winter Battery Care Checklist

**1. Get it tested before the cold arrives.**
A battery load test takes only a few minutes and tells you how much life is left. If you haven't had yours checked this fall, now is the time. Many shops, including ours, will do this quickly.

**2. Clean the terminals.**
Corrosion blocks the flow of electricity. A mixture of baking soda and water with an old toothbrush will clear light buildup. Disconnect the negative cable first and reconnect it last, and wear gloves and eye protection. If the corrosion is heavy or the cables look damaged, let a professional handle it.

**3. Check that the battery is secure.**
A loose battery vibrates, which can shorten its life and cause internal damage. Make sure the hold-down clamp is tight.

**4. Limit short trips.**
Starting the engine takes a lot of energy, and short drives don't give the alternator time to recharge the battery. Over a cold month of quick errands, the charge can slowly drain. Take a longer drive of 20 to 30 minutes once a week if you can.

**5. Turn off accessories before shutting down.**
Heaters, seat warmers, defrosters, and the stereo all draw power. Turn them off before you shut the engine off, and again before you start it, so the battery's full strength goes to starting.

**6. Park smart.**
A garage keeps the battery several degrees warmer than the outdoors. If you can't park inside, a battery blanket or a block heater can make a real difference in very cold climates.

**7. Use a maintainer if the car sits.**
If you have a vehicle that doesn't get driven often, a battery maintainer (also called a trickle charger) keeps it topped off and prevents the slow discharge that kills batteries.

## Don't Forget the Rest of the Charging System

A weak battery is sometimes a symptom, not the problem. If the alternator isn't charging properly or a parasitic drain is pulling power overnight, a new battery will fail too. When we test a battery, we also check the alternator output and the starter draw so you know the whole system is healthy.

## What to Do If You Get Stuck

If your car won't start:

1. Turn off all accessories and try again after a minute.
2. If you use jumper cables, connect them in this order: red to the dead battery's positive terminal, red to the good battery's positive terminal, black to the good battery's negative terminal, and black to unpainted metal on the dead car's engine block, away from the battery.
3. Once the car starts, let it run or drive it for at least 30 minutes.
4. Have the battery and charging system tested soon after. A jump-start fixes the symptom, not the cause.

Modern vehicles with start-stop systems or complex electronics may require a specific type of battery, such as AGM, and have specific jump-start procedures. Check your owner's manual first.
