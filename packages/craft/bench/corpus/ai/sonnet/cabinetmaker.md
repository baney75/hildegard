# Why Every Walnut Table I Build Gets Oil and Wax, Not Polyurethane

I make about thirty dining tables a year in my shop in Duluth, Georgia. Each one is walnut or another hardwood I've picked out and milled myself, and each one ends up in front of a family that will eat, argue, do homework, and celebrate around it for decades. So the finish matters to me as much as the joinery does. Here's how I finish a walnut dining table, and why.

## The Process: Three Coats of Oil, Then Wax

Once the top is sanded and ready, I apply Danish oil by hand. Three coats, with 24 hours between each. I don't rush that gap. The oil needs time to soak into the wood and cure, and shortcuts show up later as a gummy or uneven surface.

After the third coat has cured, I finish with a layer of paste wax, rubbed in by hand and buffed out. The oil works its way into the grain and brings out the depth of the walnut. The wax adds a soft protective surface and a smooth, quiet sheen you can feel when you run your hand across the top.

## Why I Don't Use Polyurethane

Film finishes like polyurethane are popular because they're fast and tough on paper. But I've never liked how they look on walnut. They sit on top of the wood like a layer of plastic, and they hide the grain you chose the board for in the first place. They also chip. Once a film finish is chipped or scratched, you can't just touch up the spot. The damage is part of a coating, and fixing it usually means stripping the whole surface.

An oil finish is part of the wood rather than a coat sitting on it. That changes how it ages, and how easily it can be repaired.

## Repairs Take an Afternoon

Say someone sets a sweating glass on the table and leaves a water ring. With an oil and wax finish, that's not a disaster. Sand the mark out with 400 grit sandpaper, re-oil the area, and you're done in an afternoon. No stripping, no refinishing the entire top, and no visible patch.

That's a big reason I recommend this finish for tables that are actually used.

## Proof From the Hollis Family Table

In 2009 I built a table for the Hollis family. It has hosted weekly dinners for fifteen years. In all that time, it has needed exactly one re-oil. It's been used hard and loved, and it still looks the way a good table should.

## Walnut Changes Over Time

If you choose walnut, expect it to darken over the first year, especially if the table gets sunlight. The color deepens and richens. It's part of the wood's character, and most of my customers come to love it.

## What It Costs

The hand-rubbed oil finish adds about $200 to the price of a table. I think it's worth it. You get a finish that looks better, feels better, and can be repaired by hand for as long as you own the table.
