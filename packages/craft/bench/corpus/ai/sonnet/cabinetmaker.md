# How I Finish a Walnut Dining Table

I build about thirty tables a year here at Oberg Woodworks in Duluth, Georgia, and every one of them ends the same way: with my hands, a rag, and a can of oil. People ask me why I don't just spray on a coat of polyurethane and call it done. It would be faster. It would also be a worse table.

## The finish, step by step

Once the walnut is sanded and the dust is off, I wipe on Danish oil by hand. Three coats, with a full 24 hours between each one. The oil soaks into the wood rather than sitting on top of it, so you end up with walnut that looks and feels like walnut, not walnut under glass. Walnut has a depth to its grain that oil brings out in a way nothing else does.

After the third coat has cured, I rub in a layer of paste wax. That gives the surface a soft sheen and a little extra protection, and it feels wonderful under your palm.

## Why not polyurethane?

I'll be blunt: a film finish like polyurethane looks like plastic, and that's because it is plastic. It sits on the surface as a hard layer. It can look fine on day one, but it chips. Once a film finish chips or wears through, you can't just touch up the spot. The whole top usually has to be stripped and redone.

An oil finish doesn't work that way, and that's the real advantage.

## Water rings? Not a crisis.

Somebody will set a sweating glass on your table. It happens at every dinner table eventually. With an oil finish, a water ring is an afternoon project, not a disaster. I sand the spot with 400 grit, wipe on fresh oil, and the mark is gone. You can do it yourself, and I'm always glad to walk customers through it.

## Proof at the Hollis table

In 2009 I built a dining table for the Hollis family. They've had weekly dinners at it for 15 years. Weekly dinners, with kids, homework, hot dishes, and everything else a family table goes through. In all that time it has needed exactly one re-oil. That's the kind of durability I'm after: not a finish that resists wear for a while and then fails, but one that ages gracefully and can always be renewed.

## What it costs

The oil finish adds about $200 to the price of a table. The extra cost is the time: three coats, a day apart, plus the waxing, all done by hand. I think it's the best $200 you can spend on a table you plan to keep for decades.

## One more thing about walnut

Walnut changes over its first year, especially if it gets sun. It darkens, and the color deepens and settles into something richer. If your table looks a little lighter when it first comes home, that's normal. Give it a year.
