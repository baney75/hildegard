# Music videos married to the song (merged from vus)

A music video succeeds when picture and music feel like one decision. Every visual choice answers to something in the song: its form, its energy curve, its imagery, its subtext. Medium is never decoration. Pencil, paper, paint, clay and pixels each say something before a single frame moves, so choose the medium for what it means.

**The bar:** would someone send this video to a friend? A video that is competent, on-beat and forgettable is a failure.

## Why music videos come out bad (read this first)

1. **Literal illustration.** The lyric says "hill," so we see a hill. Every line gets its picture and the video becomes a slideshow of the words. Show what the song means, and leave the words room.
2. **The default look for the subject.** A history song gets sepia photos with slow pans over portraits. A sad song gets rain on windows. A tech song gets glowing circuits. The subject's category is not the concept.
3. **No single image.** Nothing in the video works as a still. If no frame would make the thumbnail, there is no idea yet.
4. **Medium as gimmick.** Paper or clay chosen because it's charming, not because it says something about this song.
5. **Flat energy.** Every shot is the same intensity, so the climax has nowhere to go.
6. **Self-graded concepts.** The maker picks its own first idea. The gate is a blind critic (step 2).
7. **Guessed timing.** Cuts placed by eye on a waveform drift off the beat. Timing comes from a measured song map (step 1) and absolute frame math (step 5), never from accumulated estimates.

## Output by stage

1. **Treatment and storyboard:** one polished, self-contained HTML page (published as an Artifact when available) with a short chat handoff. It holds the concept, hero frame, visual bible, song map, shot list with timings and sync points, frame or generation budget, and production plan.
2. **Production assets,** by route:
   - **Code-built** pieces get a working project and render.
   - **AI** routes get per-shot prompts plus reference frames.
   - **Handmade** routes (drawn, painted, paper, clay, pixilation) get an **exposure sheet** (X-sheet, generated by the script in step 5), **shot cards** (time range, duration in frames, key poses, camera, lighting, materials, sync points, transition out) and a **capture checklist.**
3. **Edit, render and verify** (see Verify). Always say which is true: prepared, rendered, verified or delivered.

**Ask once, bundled.** The answers that change the work: route and who makes the frames (you, in code; an AI generator, or the user by hand), platform and aspect ratio, deadline, and budget in hours or credits. Ask them together in one question set, only for what the brief doesn't settle. If there is no answer, default to 16:9 at 1080p, 24 fps, with a 9:16 hook cutdown, built in code (Remotion when Node is available, otherwise Python with ffmpeg), and say so at the top of the treatment.

## The method

### 1. Know the song cold

**Inputs.** Get the audio and lyrics. From Suno, ask for the WAV download rather than the MP3, and the stems if the user's plan offers stem export; real stems beat separated ones, so skip `demucs` when they exist. If the song came from `mus`, its LYRICS and STYLE boxes are the brief and its **card line** (`Speaker → listener · moment · turn`) is the emotional brief. Otherwise write that card from the lyrics before anything else: who is singing to whom, where, why now, and what the hook means by the end.

**The song map** is one JSON file and the single source for Remotion, Blender, AI shot timing and exposure sheets:

- BPM, meter, key, duration.
- Beats and downbeats, measured.
- Section boundaries with timestamps (intro, verses, pre-chorus, chorus, bridge, drop, outro).
- The **energy curve:** where it rises, breaks and peaks.
- **Key moments:** stops, drops, new voices entering, the key lift, the last chorus. The biggest visual payoffs go here.
- Word timings, when type or lip sync is involved.
- **Lyric imagery and subtext:** what the words literally show and what they actually mean (in the treatment, not the JSON).

Use the `audio-sync-analysis` skill if present. Otherwise run this (`pip install librosa soundfile`), then fill sections from the `mus` tags (the script lists them in order with empty times) by matching them to the energy curve, or with a structure analyzer such as `allin1`. For word timing, force-align the known lyrics to the vocal stem (for example with `whisperx`) rather than transcribing.

```python
# usage: python3 song_map.py song.wav [lyrics.txt] > song_map.json
import json, sys, librosa, numpy as np
y, sr = librosa.load(sys.argv[1], sr=None, mono=True)
dur = librosa.get_duration(y=y, sr=sr)
tempo, beats = librosa.beat.beat_track(y=y, sr=sr, units="time")
onsets = librosa.onset.onset_detect(y=y, sr=sr, units="time", backtrack=True)
if len(beats) < 8 and len(onsets) > 8:              # tracker failed: fall back to the onset grid
    ioi = float(np.median(np.diff(onsets))); tempo = 60 / ioi
    beats = np.arange(onsets[0], dur, ioi)
    print(f"WARNING: beat tracker failed; using onset grid at {tempo:.1f} BPM. Confirm by ear.", file=sys.stderr)
if len(beats) < 8:
    sys.exit("ERROR: no usable beat grid. Supply the BPM from Suno/the DAW and build the grid by hand.")
rms = librosa.feature.rms(y=y)[0]; t = librosa.times_like(rms, sr=sr)
step = max(1, len(rms) // int(dur * 2))           # energy sampled ~2x per second
tags = [l.strip() for l in open(sys.argv[2]) if l.strip().startswith("[")] if len(sys.argv) > 2 else []
print(json.dumps({
    "file": sys.argv[1], "duration": round(dur, 3),
    "bpm": round(float(np.atleast_1d(tempo)[0]), 2), "meter": 4,
    "beats": [round(float(b), 3) for b in beats],
    "downbeats": [round(float(b), 3) for b in beats[::4]],   # assumes beat 1 = first detected; check by ear
    "onsets": [round(float(o), 3) for o in onsets],
    "energy": [[round(float(t[i]), 2), round(float(rms[i]), 4)] for i in range(0, len(rms), step)],
    "sections": [{"tag": s, "start": None, "end": None} for s in tags],
    "words": []                                                # [{"word":..., "start":..., "end":...}]
}, indent=1))
```

Compare the measured BPM with the BPM in the `mus` STYLE box; a result near half or double is the tracker's octave error, so correct it. You cannot hear the audio, so verify timing mechanically: render a ten-second sync-test clip that flashes on beats and labels section starts, and check it against the onset data. Hand the by-ear check to the user and report sync as unchecked until they confirm.

### 2. Find the conceit

Generate four distinct concepts. Each concept card has:

- **One sentence:** the whole idea. If it can't be said in one sentence, it isn't an idea yet.
- **The hero frame:** the single image people will remember and the thumbnail will use, described precisely (subject, framing, light, medium).
- **The turn:** how the song's turn shows on screen, usually as a motif that returns transformed.
- **Medium and why:** what the medium says about this song, in one clause.

At least two concepts must not illustrate the lyric, and at least one must avoid the default look for the subject. The main types:

- **Performance:** the artist sings or plays. Honesty and presence carry it.
- **Narrative:** a story runs parallel to the song, or in counterpoint to it.
- **Concept or conceit:** one strong rule executed relentlessly (one take, everything made of paper, the world rewinds).
- **Visual music or abstract:** shape, color and motion embody the sound itself.
- **Lyric or kinetic type:** the words are the picture.
- **Choreography:** bodies as the instrument.
- **Documentary or found footage:** real images recontextualized.
- **Hybrid:** for example a live-action performer walking into a drawn world.

Choose between **parallel** (visuals illustrate the lyric) and **counterpoint** (visuals create tension with it, so a cheerful song over a bleak image reads as irony).

**Blind critic (mandatory before anything expensive).** When the Agent tool exists, spawn one fresh agent and give it only the lyrics and the four concept cards, unlabeled and in shuffled order, with this prompt:

> You are a blunt music-video commissioner. For each concept: would you greenlight it, yes or no, in one line; is the hero frame striking as a still; and does it illustrate the words or reveal the meaning? Then pick one, name its biggest risk, and say the one change that would make it better. No praise padding.

Take the pick unless you can say exactly why the critic is wrong, and fold in the change. One round; don't spawn duplicate critics. Without an Agent tool, attack the concepts yourself and write down each one's biggest weakness before choosing.

**Hero frame test.** Before storyboarding, make the hero frame: a drawing, a generated still, or a rendered test. Look at it. If it isn't striking as a still, revise the concept or take the runner-up. When production is expensive (handmade frames, long renders, paid credits), show the user the hero frame with the one-sentence pitch and wait for a go.

### 3. Research and references

Study canonical work for technique, never to copy it:

- **Pencil rotoscope merged with live action:** a-ha, "Take On Me" (dir. Steve Barron, 1985). A performer crosses between photographed and drawn worlds.
- **Stop-motion, pixilation and clay in one piece:** Peter Gabriel, "Sledgehammer" (dir. Stephen R. Johnson, with Aardman, 1986). Every visual change is timed to a musical shift, and the fingerprints are left in.
- **Pixilation from stills:** Oren Lavie, "Her Morning Elegance" (2009). A whole dream built from photographed poses.
- **Structure mapped to instruments:** Michel Gondry's work for Chemical Brothers ("Star Guitar"), where passing scenery is placed so each landscape element hits a specific sound, and for Daft Punk ("Around the World"), where each dancer group embodies one instrument.
- **One-take choreography:** OK Go, "Here It Goes Again" (2006).
- **Camera-less capture:** Radiohead, "House of Cards" (dir. James Frost, 2008), made from laser-scanned data with no cameras.
- **Archival and present intercut:** Johnny Cash, "Hurt" (dir. Mark Romanek, 2003).
- **An animated band as identity:** Gorillaz.
- **Visual music lineage:** Oskar Fischinger's abstract films; Len Lye's *A Colour Box* (1935), painted directly on film; Norman McLaren, who drew directly on celluloid, including the soundtrack; Lotte Reiniger's cut-paper silhouettes; Terry Gilliam's cut-out collage; Aleksandr Petrov's paint-on-glass.

When the subject involves real history, places, scripture or people, verify facts and imagery the same way `mus` does. Accuracy is the floor, not the concept: use the real object, place or document as the motif (the riding crop, the hilltop rock, the letter), not a slideshow of portraits. Look up current tools and model capabilities before recommending them; they change monthly.

### 4. Write the visual bible

Define it once and enforce it everywhere:

- **Palette:** three to five colors, with an emotional reason for each, and how the palette shifts with the song.
- **Texture:** paper grain, line boil, brush, film grain or clean vector.
- **Line and shape language:** round or angular, thick or thin.
- **Frame rate feel:** on ones (smooth), on twos (classic hand-made), on threes or fours (choppy, raw).
- **Aspect ratio and platform:** 16:9 for YouTube, 9:16 for Shorts, Reels and TikTok, 1:1 for feeds. Plan safe areas and cutdowns from the start.
- **Type**, if used: typeface, hierarchy, motion behavior.
- **Motifs:** one or two recurring images that return transformed at the climax.
- **Rules:** what never happens in this world. Constraints create style.

### 5. Do the beat math

```
frames per beat = fps × 60 / BPM
frames per bar  = frames per beat × beats per bar
```

At 24 fps and 120 BPM, a beat is 12 frames and a 4/4 bar is 48. At 24 fps and 90 BPM, a beat is 16. Fractional results (24 fps at 100 BPM gives 14.4) are fine: place every event at `round(time × fps)` from the song map's absolute times, so the error never exceeds half a frame, or pick a frame rate that divides evenly (25 fps at 100 BPM gives 15). Never accumulate rounded beat lengths, because the drift adds up.

For handmade routes, generate the exposure sheet straight from the song map (one row per frame; fill action, camera and hold by hand or in the shot cards):

```python
# usage: python3 xsheet.py song_map.json FPS > xsheet.csv
import csv, json, sys
m, fps = json.load(open(sys.argv[1])), int(sys.argv[2])
nframes = round(m["duration"] * fps)
beat_f = {round(b * fps): i + 1 for i, b in enumerate(m["beats"])}   # absolute rounding: no drift
down_f = {round(b * fps) for b in m["downbeats"]}
word_f = {round(w["start"] * fps): w["word"] for w in m.get("words", [])}
sec_f = {round(s["start"] * fps): s["tag"] for s in m["sections"] if s.get("start") is not None}
w = csv.writer(sys.stdout)
w.writerow(["frame", "timecode", "section", "beat", "downbeat", "lyric", "action", "camera", "hold"])
for f in range(nframes):
    s = f / fps; tc = f"{int(s//60):02d}:{int(s%60):02d}:{f%fps:02d}"
    w.writerow([f + 1, tc, sec_f.get(f, ""), beat_f.get(f, ""), "X" if f in down_f else "", word_f.get(f, ""), "", "", ""])
```

**Sync hierarchy** (use deliberately; syncing everything to everything becomes "Mickey-Mousing" and tires the eye):

- **Section level:** scene or world changes on section boundaries.
- **Bar or phrase level:** cuts and camera moves on downbeats.
- **Beat level:** accents on kicks and snares, used selectively.
- **Sub-beat level:** texture, flicker, line boil and particles.
- **Word level:** type and lip sync.

**Mirror the energy curve.** Visual intensity rises and falls with the song, and the single biggest visual payoff goes on the song's biggest moment. A stop or silence wants a freeze, a cut to black or stillness. Let long held notes hold the shot.

### 6. Storyboard and shot list

Use the `animation-storyboard` skill if present. Each shot gets: time range tied to the song map, section, lyric, what happens (something must change), camera, medium notes, sync points, and the transition into the next shot. Thumbnail key frames, as rough drawings or generated stills, before anything expensive. Plot planned visual intensity per shot against the song map's energy curve in the treatment; the two lines should rise and fall together, with the peak shot on the peak.

### 7. Budget reality

A three-minute song at 12 drawings per second is about 2,160 drawings. Make it feasible with **holds** on long notes; **cycles** (walks, loops, boil); **limited animation**, where only the mouth, eyes or one element moves; animating on threes for raw sections; **reusing** shots in repeated choruses with a variation; **mixing** live action with drawn accents; and saving full animation for the peak. For AI routes, budget generations as shots × 3 takes. State the budget (drawings, frames, shots or credits) in the treatment before production starts.

## Production by medium

### Pencil and hand-drawn 2D

Draw on paper with a lightbox and peg bar or corner registration marks, or digitally in Procreate Dreams, TVPaint, Krita, OpenToonz or Blender Grease Pencil. Animate on twos by default. Treat **line boil** (re-tracing the same pose two or three times and cycling them) as living texture on holds. Use smears and multiples for fast action. For **singing characters**, use a mouth chart (the classic Preston Blair phoneme set). Rhubarb Lip Sync can generate mouth cues from the vocal stem; then hand-correct the key words. For paper scans: consistent lighting, 300 dpi, registration preserved, and levels cleaned so the paper tone matches frame to frame.

### Rotoscope

Shoot reference video at the final framing, then trace selected frames on twos or threes. Decide how much to trace: full figure, outline only, or a mix. To blend with live action, match grain and color. EbSynth can propagate a few painted keyframes across live footage for a painterly rotoscope look. Lock the camera or track it.

### Paint

- **Paint-on-glass:** paint under a fixed camera, shoot, wipe and modify, shoot again. The smear trails become the motion.
- **Painted frames:** oil, gouache or watercolor on numbered cards. Very slow, so reserve it for key shots or work on fours.
- **Digital paint:** textured brushes plus paper overlays. Keep brush texture stable frame to frame or let it boil on purpose.
- **Painterly from footage:** paint keyframes and propagate them (EbSynth or similar), then hand-fix the drift.

### Scrap paper, cutout and collage

Use jointed cutouts (brass fasteners or tape hinges), replacement pieces for mouths and hands, and torn edges kept visible. Stack sheets of glass at different heights for multiplane depth under a top-down camera. For silhouettes, backlight black card (Reiniger). For magazine collage, mind source-image copyright; use your own photos, public domain or licensed material. Digitally: scan pieces and rig them in After Effects, Cavalry, Moho or Remotion with the real paper texture kept. Animate cutouts with slight imperfection, never perfectly smooth.

### Stop-motion, clay and object animation

Capture with Dragonframe (pro) or Stop Motion Studio (accessible). Set exposure, white balance, focus and ISO to **manual**, because auto settings cause flicker. Block window light and use constant lights. Lock the camera down. Use onion skin. Shoot 12 frames per second of screen time (on twos in a 24 fps timeline). Plan armatures, rigs and rig removal. Deflicker in post is a last resort, not a plan. Keep the fingerprints if the world allows them.

### Pixilation

People as stop-motion puppets. Shoot stills of held poses, moving people or camera a step at a time. Use a tripod or floor registration marks, plan the moves on the beat grid (one pose per beat or half-beat; the X-sheet's beat column is the shooting script), and use burst capture for hands-free work. Sliding a person across a floor frame by frame produces the classic gliding effect.

### Direct, scratch and generative abstract

Scratch or paint on film or clear leader, or build the digital equivalent: canvas, p5.js or shaders driven by audio features. Map bands to parameters: bass to scale or camera shake, mids to color, highs to particles and flicker. Use onsets for discrete events and smoothed envelopes (fast attack, slow release) for continuous motion. With stems, drive each layer from its own stem instead of splitting one mix by frequency.

### 3D

Blender (Eevee for speed, Cycles for realism), or whatever the user has. Pick a look: low-poly, toon-shaded (Grease Pencil and line art for a hand-drawn feel), clay-like, or photoreal. Plan camera language as a cinematographer would. Drive animation from audio with Graph Editor › Channel › Sound to Samples (then Samples to Keys for editable keys), and drive Geometry Nodes inputs from that curve; Geometry Nodes has no native audio input. Or import the song map JSON with a short Python script and keyframe on its beats. Check the user's Blender version first, since menus move. Budget render time per frame before committing to length.

### Motion graphics, kinetic type and lyric videos

Use the Remotion skills when present (`remotion-create`, `remotion-best-practices`, `motion-design-rules`, `remotion-captions`). Load the song map JSON as the timing source. Land each word on its timestamp, keep it on screen long enough to read (about 0.3 s minimum per short word, longer for phrases), use one emphasis treatment for key words, respect safe areas, and keep everything legible on a phone.

### Live action

Shoot performance coverage (wide, medium, close-up, detail) for every section. **Play back the track on set** so lip sync is real. For slow-motion lip sync, play the song at (capture fps ÷ timeline fps) times normal speed while filming at the capture rate: 48 into 24 is 2×, 120 into 24 is 5×. Then conform to the timeline rate. Time-stretch the playback to keep pitch if the performer struggles. A one-take piece needs rehearsal against the track and timing marks on the floor. Phone shooting: lock exposure and focus, shoot at 24 or 30 fps consistently, and use window light or a cheap LED with diffusion.

### AI-generated video

The landscape changes monthly, so check current models before recommending one. As of mid-October 2026, leading options include Veo 3.1, Seedance 2 and 2.5, Kling 3.0 (4.0 rolling out), Runway Gen-4.5, Luma Ray 3.2, Grok Imagine and MiniMax H3 (open weights). Clips run roughly 5–15 seconds, and several models generate audio or lip sync. Mute model-generated audio: the master track is the only audio. Use Hildegard's `direct_shot` on the user's own GPUs when it is connected, or another generator the user has connected.

- **Consistency:** make a character and style reference sheet first, generate stills, then animate image-to-video with start and end frames. Repeat the same style phrases and seed where supported.
- **Prompt anatomy:** subject, action, camera move and lens, lighting, medium and style, motion quality, duration.
- **Timing:** generate each shot slightly longer than its song-map duration, then cut on the beat in the edit.
- **Hybrid wins:** AI backgrounds with hand-made foregrounds, or real footage stylized from keyframes.
- **Limits:** no real people's likeness without consent, no living artists' named styles, no copyrighted characters. Disclose AI use where platforms require it (YouTube's altered or synthetic content label, for example).

### Found footage and archival

Draw on public domain and openly licensed sources: Prelinger Archives and the Internet Archive, NASA imagery, Library of Congress collections and Wikimedia Commons. Verify the license per clip and keep a source list. Recontextualize: the cut creates the meaning.

### More forms

- **Interactive web music video:** the viewer steers, chooses or explores while the song plays. Build it as one HTML page and publish it as an Artifact, in the lineage of *The Wilderness Downtown*. Lock interaction to the audio element's clock, not to frame timers.
- **Game engine and machinima:** Unreal or Unity for real-time worlds, captured cameras and fast iteration.
- **Brickfilm:** stop-motion with toy bricks and figures. Same capture rules as stop-motion.
- **Sand, puppetry and shadow puppetry:** sand on a lightbox animated under the camera; rod or string puppets shot live; shadow puppets backlit through a screen.
- **Light painting and long exposure:** stop-motion where each frame is a long exposure drawn with lights.
- **Projection mapping:** animation projected onto buildings, objects or the performer, then filmed.
- **Live session or multicam concert:** three or more angles synced to the master track, edited to the music like any other piece.
- **360 and VR:** equirectangular framing, where the viewer chooses the shot. Direct attention with sound and light, not cuts.
- **Spotify Canvas:** a 3–8 second vertical loop that plays behind the track. Make it seamless: the last frame must flow into the first, and the loop length should be a whole number of bars.

### Visualizers and minimum viable

For a fast release, use an audio-reactive loop, an animated cover (Suno's cover-art animate tool loops a single image), or one strong still with slow motion and type. Ship that, then build the full video.

## Edit and finish

- Cut to the music: on downbeats, before or at the phrase, never randomly mid-beat unless the jolt is the point.
- **Unify mixed media** with one grade, one grain or paper layer, and consistent black and white points.
- Titles and credits: the artist, song, director and makers. Credit everyone who drew a frame.
- **Export:** H.264 MP4, consistent frame rate end to end (24, 25 or 30 fps; watch for 23.976 vs 24 and 29.97 vs 30 mismatches between sources and timeline), 1080p or 4K, 48 kHz AAC at 320 kbps or PCM. Encode drafts at `-preset veryfast`; slower presets only for the final master. Make vertical cutdowns built around the hook. Check each platform's current length and spec limits before export.
- **Rights:** the song must be the user's or licensed. For Suno songs, ownership and commercial use depend on the plan the song was made under; check before release.

## Verify (a check that could fail)

Use the `render-inspect-verify` skill if present. Otherwise:

1. `ffprobe` the output: resolution, fps, duration matching the song within one frame, audio stream present.
2. View a contact sheet of the whole piece (`ffmpeg -i out.mp4 -vf "fps=1/4,scale=320:-1,tile=6x5" sheet.png`). Look for blank frames, wrong shots, broken transitions and whether intensity visibly escalates.
3. Spot-check sync at five points (a section change, the biggest hit, two lyric words, the final hit) by extracting the frame at each song-map time. Keep drift within about a frame. Viewers notice sound arriving before the picture sooner than sound arriving after it (ITU-R BT.1359), so never let audio lead.
4. Crop and read every line of on-screen text at full resolution, and once at phone size.
5. **Fresh-eyes check on the finished cut** (when the Agent tool exists): give one fresh agent only the contact sheet and the lyrics, and ask: "Which frame is the thumbnail? Does the picture escalate with the song? Name the weakest stretch and why. Would you send this to a friend?" Fix what it finds or say why not. One round.
6. Hand the full watch-through, with sound and at full screen, to the user. Report by-ear sync and feel as unchecked until they confirm.

Report what was checked, what passed, what failed and what remains unchecked.

## The science, briefly

- **Cross-modal correspondences** are shared across most people (Spence, 2011): high pitch reads as up, small and bright; low pitch as down, large and dark; loudness as size; fast tempo as fast motion. → Map visuals the way ears already expect, then break the mapping only for effect.
- **Event boundaries:** viewers segment experience into events, and cuts at natural event boundaries go unnoticed. → Musical phrase ends are event boundaries, so cut there and transitions disappear.
- **Attention follows motion and contrast.** → Only one thing should move fast at a time, and the eye should always know where to look.

## The art, briefly

- **One image people remember.** Design the frame that becomes the thumbnail and the memory.
- **Motif and payoff.** Plant an image early and transform it at the climax.
- **Escalation.** The video should grow as the song grows. Don't spend everything in the first chorus.
- **Imperfection is warmth.** Boil, fingerprints, torn edges and hand-shake signal a person made this. Keep them when the song is human.
- **Restraint.** Stillness against a loud moment can hit harder than motion.
- **Serve the song.** If a shot is cool but fights the music, cut it.
- **Example of the difference.** For a widow's waltz about Strong Vincent (Gettysburg, 1863), the default treatment is sepia portraits, cannon smoke and a slow push on his photograph. A concept with an idea: one continuous shot of an old woman's hands on a table, oiling and wrapping a riding crop while the seasons change in the window behind her. The hilltop appears only once, at the bridge, reflected in the window glass. The hero frame is her hands and the crop. The turn is that in the last chorus she hangs the crop back on her own saddle.
