# Writing craft: fiction, poetry, scripts, lyrics, prayer, speech

Load this for any creative writing. For essays, nonfiction, research and academic papers, load `nonfiction-academic.md` as well. For whole books, add `books.md`. Songs for Suno go to `song-suno.md`. The original "5e. Writing" guidance follows the new material at the end of this file.

## Sources (what to take from each)

- **Ursula K. Le Guin, *Steering the Craft* (2015 ed.):** the sound of prose, sentence length, point of view and voice, with exercises. Read sentences aloud.
- **John Gardner, *The Art of Fiction* (1983):** fiction as a "vivid and continuous dream." Anything that breaks the dream (vagueness, clumsiness, authorial intrusion) is a fault.
- **Robert McKee, *Story* (1997):**
  - each scene turns a value from positive to negative or the reverse;
  - a story is a chain of turning scenes;
  - the gap opens between what a character expects and what happens.
- **John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story* (2007):** desire, need, opponent, plan, battle, self-revelation. The moral argument is built into the structure.
- **Anton Chekhov's letters:** show the specific detail ("the glitter of light on a broken bottle"). Every element must pay off (Chekhov's gun).
- **Flannery O'Connor, *Mystery and Manners* (1969):** fiction works through the senses; the concrete carries the spiritual.
- **Stephen King, *On Writing* (2000):** write the first draft with the door closed and the second with it open. The second draft is the first one minus 10%.
- **George Saunders, *A Swim in a Pond in the Rain* (2021):** read the Russian masters closely; ask, line by line, what the reader expects now, and what they need next.
- **Poetry:**
  - Mary Oliver's *A Poetry Handbook* (1994): sound, line, imagery;
  - Stephen Fry's *The Ode Less Travelled* (2005): meter and forms;
  - Ezra Pound's "A Few Don'ts by an Imagiste" (1913): go in fear of abstractions; use no superfluous word;
  - Gerard Manley Hopkins's sprung rhythm.
- **Screenwriting:**
  - William Goldman, *Adventures in the Screen Trade* (1983): "Nobody knows anything"; enter scenes late and leave early;
  - Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat* (2005): the beat sheet, a useful checklist, not a formula;
  - industry-standard format (Courier 12 pt, about one page per minute); use Fountain markup, then convert to PDF.
- **Speech and rhetoric:** Aristotle's *Rhetoric* (ethos, pathos, logos); Lincoln's Gettysburg Address as the model of brevity; Peggy Noonan's *On Speaking Well* (1998).
- **Sacred and liturgical writing:**
  - the Psalms (parallelism, lament and praise);
  - the collect form of the Book of Common Prayer (address, attribution, petition, purpose, doxology);
  - hymn meters (CM 8.6.8.6, LM 8.8.8.8, SM 6.6.8.6).

## Fiction

- **Desire and obstacle.** A character wants something specific, now, against something specific. No desire, no story.
- **Scenes turn.** Every scene changes a value (safe to endangered, ignorant to aware). A scene where nothing turns is summary in disguise; cut it or compress it (McKee).
- **Point of view** is a contract: first person, close third, omniscient. Choose it for what the reader must and mustn't know, and break it only on purpose.
- **Scene and summary.** Dramatize what matters, summarize what connects. Most beginners do the opposite.
- **Dialogue:**
  - people talk past each other, interrupt and avoid;
  - subtext over text;
  - "said" is invisible, so use it;
  - cut greetings and small talk unless they do work.
- **Detail:** the one specific, surprising, sensory detail that stands for the whole (Chekhov). Three generic details are worse than one true one.
- **Openings** start in motion: a character, a situation, a question. **Endings** resonate: an image, an action, a line that recasts the whole, never a moral.
- **Revision passes:** structure (scenes, turns, order), then character (desire, change), then scene (enter late, leave early), then line (cut 10–20%, sound aloud).

## Poetry

- **The line is the unit of attention.** Line breaks create emphasis, delay and double meaning.
- **Image over statement** (Pound): "the apparition of these faces in the crowd" does what "people looked sad" cannot.
- **Sound:** meter (scan it with `pronouncing`), rhyme as a choice, assonance, alliteration, the weight of stressed syllables.
- **Forms carry meaning:**
  - the sonnet's turn (the volta);
  - the villanelle's obsession;
  - the ghazal's couplets returning to one word;
  - haiku's cut (*kireji*);
  - the psalm's parallelism;
  - free verse's breath.
- **Compression:** every word does work. Read each line alone and ask what it adds (the bar's near reading).

## Scripts (film, video, audio drama)

- **Write only what can be seen and heard.** No thoughts in action lines unless they become action.
- **Action lines** are short, present tense and visual: one camera-worthy beat per paragraph.
- **Scenes start late and leave early.** Each scene turns (see Fiction).
- **Format:** standard screenplay format. Write in Fountain markup, then convert to a PDF in Courier 12 (with `afterwriting` or `screenplain`). For audio drama, cue sound effects and music in caps on their own lines.
- **For a music video or short,** the script is the treatment and the shot list (`music-video.md`, `drafting-storyboards.md`).

## Prayer, liturgy, devotional writing

- Address God as the tradition the user belongs to addresses Him, in their voice, verified against Scripture and their tradition's texts (pitfall 19).
- **The collect:** address ("Almighty God"), attribution ("who…"), petition ("grant…"), purpose ("that…"), and doxology ("through Jesus Christ our Lord").
- **Lament** (Psalm 13, Psalm 88): complaint, then petition, then trust, then praise. Lament may end unresolved (Psalm 88). Don't force consolation the text doesn't give.
- **Quote Scripture exactly** from a named translation (the KJV is public domain); check every reference.

## Line-level craft (every genre)

- **Concrete nouns and strong verbs.** Cut adverbs that prop up weak verbs.
- **Vary sentence length:** long sentences build, short ones land.
- **The machine-voice purge** (SKILL.md 5e): stock phrases, "tapestry," "testament," "delve," "in a world where," reflexive groups of three, "not X, but Y" scaffolding, em-dash habits, and abstractions doing work images should do.
- **Read it aloud.** The ear catches what the eye forgives.



## Original 5e notes: forms, craft and process

Writing is an art like the others: it has a far reading and a near reading, a form, a style and a voice.

**Choose the form for its meaning:**
- free verse, or meter (iambic pentameter; hymn meters CM 8.6.8.6 and LM 8.8.8.8);
- the sonnet and its turn (volta);
- the psalm's parallelism;
- haiku's cut;
- the parable's single reversal;
- the essay's argument;
- prayer and liturgy (address, petition, doxology);
- lyrics (5h, `song-suno.md`);
- a script, with action lines that can be filmed.

**Craft:**
- Concrete nouns and strong verbs; images over abstractions (Pound); the thing shown, not named.
- Every word earns its place.
- Sound used on purpose: assonance, alliteration, rhyme as a choice, not a reflex.
- Line breaks that carry meaning.
- One clear voice. When writing as the user, use their voice and words.
- An ending that lands or opens. Never a moral tag explaining the piece.
- The up-close layer: an acrostic, a refrain that changes meaning on its return, a buried scripture echo, a number structure (three stanzas, seven lines).

**Process:**
1. Draft fast.
2. Revise in passes: structure, then image, then sound, then cut 10–20%.
3. For meter, scan with code: the `pronouncing` library (CMU dictionary) gives syllable counts and stresses.
4. Run a cliche and machine-voice pass, and remove:
   - stock phrases;
   - "tapestry", "testament", "delve", "in a world where";
   - reflexive groups of three;
   - "not X, but Y" scaffolding;
   - em-dash habits;
   - abstract nouns doing the work images should do.
5. Quote scripture exactly, from the named translation.


## Checks (from the original step 6)

- Re-read it twice: once whole, for the far reading, and once line by line, for the near reading.
- Scan the meter with code if the piece is metered.
- Run the cliche pass.
- Check every quotation against its source.
- For lyrics, check syllable counts against the melody.
